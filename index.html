About this exercise

This is a learning exercise only. The seed phrase you make here is not for holding (self-custody of) real bitcoin. This page runs in a web browser on a computer that is connected to the internet, and anything typed on an online computer could be seen by malware or other software.

Real keys can be made completely offline: on a computer that has never been connected to the internet, or entirely by hand with dice, pencil, paper and the same math you'll do here.

The point of this exercise is to walk you through, step by step, what a hardware wallet or wallet software does inside when it creates your keys. A wallet does all of this in a fraction of a second. Here you do it slowly enough to see every step: randomness → bits → checksum → numbers → words.

⚠ Read this first

  • Never share your 24 words. Anyone who has your words owns the bitcoin, with no way to get it back. No real company, wallet, exchange or support person will ever ask for them.
  • Use real dice. Don't make up your bits. People are bad at being random. Bits you pick in your head, patterns (like 0101…), or all 0s or all 1s make words that thieves can guess. Thieves run programs that constantly check weak, guessable keys and empty any bitcoin they find.

Step 1Roll your dice and type your bits

What's happening: every Bitcoin key starts as a big random number. A wallet gets its randomness from a hardware random number generator inside its chip; you'll get yours from dice, which you can see. Each bit is a coin-flip choice (0 or 1), and 256 of them give 2256 possible keys, a 78-digit number, close to the estimated number of atoms in the observable universe. That's why nobody can guess a truly random key, and why made-up or patterned bits are dangerous.

Read your dice left to right: 1, 2 or 3 = 0  ·  4, 5 or 6 = 1. Type one bit per yellow box: 11 per row for rows 1–23, then 3 in row 24. Each row is split 4 – 4 – 3 to help you keep your place. The orange boxes are the checksum. You'll get them in Step 2.

Tip: the cursor jumps to the next box by itself. You can also paste a string of 0s and 1s into any box.

Step 2Find the checksum

What's happening: your 256 bits get run through SHA-256, a "hash" function. It always gives the same 64-character answer for the same input, but change even one bit and the answer changes completely, and there's no way to work backward from the answer to the input. The BIP-39 standard takes the first 8 bits of that hash (the first two hex characters) and adds them to the end of your bits. That's the checksum: if someone later writes down a wrong word, the checksum won't match and the wallet will warn them. 256 + 8 = 264 bits, which divides evenly into 24 groups of 11.

Step 3Turn your bits into words

What's happening: 11 bits can make 211 = 2048 different numbers, from 0 to 2047, and the BIP-39 word list has exactly 2048 words. So each group of 11 bits picks one word. Reading a group of bits as a number works like reading a regular number, only each place is worth double the one to its right instead of ten times: 1, 2, 4, 8 … 1024. The words don't add anything new. They're just an easier way for people to write down and read back the same 264 bits without mistakes.

Add up the place values that have a 1 under them = M. The word list starts at line 1, but the numbers start at 0, so add 1 = N. Line N of the BIP-39 word list is your word.

Your 24 words

Your words appear here as each row is finished.

What a wallet does next: it runs your 24 words (plus an optional extra passphrase) through a function called PBKDF2, 2048 rounds of HMAC-SHA512, to make a 512-bit "seed." From that seed it builds a master private key (BIP-32), and from the master key it makes all your addresses. Those steps are too long to do by hand, but they're the same every time, so the same 24 words always lead back to the same bitcoin.
